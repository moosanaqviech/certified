AWS Disaster Recovery: Backup and Restore, Pilot Light, Warm Standby or Active/Active

AWS describes four disaster recovery strategies, from cheapest and slowest to costliest and fastest: backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby and multi-site active/active. You choose by the recovery point objective (RPO), the recovery time objective (RTO) and the budget. The cheapest strategy that meets both objectives is the right one.

Last updated October 2026.

Two numbers decide everything

  • RPO is the most data the business can afford to lose, measured as time since the last recovery point.
  • RTO is the longest the business can wait for service to come back.

Both are business decisions. Lower values cost more, so a question that gives generous objectives and asks for the lowest cost is pointing at the cheaper end of the scale, even if a faster strategy would also work.

The four strategies

Backup and restore

Only backups exist in the recovery Region: snapshots, AWS Backup cross-Region copies, S3 replication. After a disaster you redeploy the infrastructure and restore the data. AWS stresses infrastructure as code (AWS CloudFormation or the AWS CDK) here, because rebuilding by hand is slow and error-prone and can blow the RTO. RPO is set by how often you back up.

Pilot light

Data replicates continuously to live data stores in the recovery Region, and core infrastructure is in place, but application servers are switched off. AWS's best practice for "switched off" is not to deploy them at all until needed. On failover you switch them on and scale out. AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery uses this pattern for server-based workloads.

Warm standby

A scaled-down but fully functional copy of production runs in the recovery Region and can take traffic immediately at reduced capacity. Failover means scaling up, so service quotas in that Region must already be high enough to reach production size.

Multi-site active/active

The workload runs and serves users in two or more Regions, routed by Amazon Route 53 or AWS Global Accelerator. AWS calls it the most complex and costly approach, able to bring recovery time near zero. Its variant, hot standby, runs full capacity in the second Region but sends it no traffic until failover.

Pilot light vs warm standby: the pair people mix up

AWS's own whitepaper admits the difference can be hard to see, then states it plainly: pilot light cannot process requests without action first, because servers must be turned on; warm standby can handle traffic immediately, at reduced capacity, and only needs to scale up. If a question says "must serve requests immediately after failover, even at lower capacity", pilot light is out.

Wording that picks each strategy

Backup and restore

"Lowest cost", "can be down for a day", "protect against deletion or corruption".

Pilot light

"Replicate data continuously but keep compute costs minimal", "no servers running until a disaster".

Warm standby

"Serve traffic immediately, even at reduced capacity", "scale up after failover".

Active/active

"Near-zero downtime and data loss", "users in several Regions served every day", "cost is secondary".

Replication is not backup. Continuous replication copies a corruption or a malicious delete as faithfully as good data. AWS's guidance is that every strategy, active/active included, still keeps point-in-time backups, and that failover should rely on data plane operations such as Amazon Application Recovery Controller routing controls rather than control plane changes.

FAQ

What is the difference between pilot light and warm standby?

Pilot light keeps data replicated but application servers off, so it cannot serve requests until servers are started. Warm standby runs a smaller, fully working copy that serves traffic immediately and only needs to scale up, so it recovers faster and costs more.

Which AWS disaster recovery strategy is the cheapest?

Backup and restore is the cheapest because only backups are stored in the recovery Region. It also has the highest RPO and RTO, since infrastructure must be redeployed and data restored after the disaster.

What is RPO and RTO in AWS?

RPO, the recovery point objective, is the most data a business can afford to lose, measured in time. RTO, the recovery time objective, is the longest it can wait for the service to be restored. Lower values need costlier strategies.

Which DR strategy does AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery use?

AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery uses the pilot light strategy. It replicates servers at block level into a staging area and launches full-capacity recovery instances only when failover is triggered.

Does multi-site active/active still need backups?

Yes. Replication between active Regions also replicates corruption and deletions, so point-in-time backups remain the only way back from a data disaster. AWS notes that recovery from data corruption always has a recovery point before the problem was found.
